Water around a water heater is one of the most common calls we get, and the first question is always the same: where is it actually coming from? Water runs downhill and pools at the lowest point, so the puddle is often nowhere near the source. It might be the temperature and pressure relief valve or its discharge line, the drain valve near the bottom of the tank, the hot and cold connections on top, condensation on a cold tank, or a nearby pipe that has nothing to do with the heater. It might also be the tank itself, and that answer changes everything. In Huntington, WV, we trace the source before we touch a wrench.

Our diagnosis is methodical. We dry the area, look at the T&P valve and the end of its discharge pipe, inspect the drain valve, and follow the hot and cold lines up to the fittings on top of the tank. On gas units, we look under the burner access for water, which can point to condensation or, more seriously, a failing tank. If the relief valve has been discharging, we measure static water pressure and check for thermal expansion, since a closed plumbing system without an expansion tank can spike pressure every time the burner runs. Only then do we recommend a fix.

The repair depends on what we found. Valve and fitting leaks are the good news: relief valves, drain valves, dielectric nipples, and flex connectors are stocked on our trucks, and we can often add an expansion tank or pressure-reducing valve the same day if pressure caused the problem. A leak from the tank body is the bad news, because a rusted-through tank is past saving, and we will say so rather than sell a patch. In both cases, we help limit future damage with a properly drained pan, a leak alarm or automatic shutoff, and a quick lesson on your shutoff valve. When to Call for Water Heater Leak Repair

Catching a symptom early is often the difference between a quick part swap and a tank that gives out on the garage floor. Here are the most common indicators that it's time to call a professional:

  • Water inside the burner compartment of a gas heater
  • Water dripping from the relief valve or out of the end of its discharge pipe
  • A drain valve at the bottom of the tank that drips even when closed
  • A wet floor near the water heater that keeps coming back
  • A water bill that rises without any change in usage
  • A musty odor or soft floor around the heater location
  • Rust-tinted hot water and signs of seepage near the base
  • Corroded pipe connections or damp flex lines above the heater

The Benefits of Professional Water Heater Leak Repair

Reading a water heater leak correctly is harder than it looks. A drip that appears to come from the bottom may start at a fitting on top and run down the jacket, and condensation on a cold tank can pass for a failing tank. Replacing the wrong part wastes money, and overlooking a tank that is starting to go can lead to a much bigger flood later. There are safety concerns, too. The relief valve guards the tank against runaway heat and pressure, and blocking it to stop a drip can set up a violent failure. Tightening corroded fittings on an old tank can crack them, and electric heaters sit on 240-volt circuits that do not mix well with wet floors.

With a trained eye, the answer usually comes quickly. We isolate each possible source in turn, confirm it with fresh water on a dried surface, and measure static pressure so the fix actually lasts. Worn relief valves, weeping drain valves, and corroded connections are swapped the same visit, and pressure problems get a real solution, whether that is an expansion tank or a pressure-reducing valve. A rusted-through tank gets an honest replacement recommendation rather than a patch, and we can add a piped drain pan and a leak sensor to protect your home going forward.

Water Heater Care Tips From Our Technicians

A few simple habits, like an annual flush and a quick look at the relief valve, can add years to a tank. Follow these tips from our technicians:

  • Flush the tank once a year so sediment does not overheat the bottom and speed corrosion
  • Make sure a closed plumbing system has a working expansion tank to absorb heated water
  • Make a habit of checking around the heater for moisture or corrosion every few weeks
  • Add a floor sensor or an automatic shutoff valve that closes the supply when it detects water
  • Have household pressure measured, since high pressure wears out relief valves and fittings faster
  • Have the anode rod checked every few years; once it is used up, the tank starts to corrode
  • Place the heater in a drain pan with a line to a floor drain or outdoors, especially above finished space
