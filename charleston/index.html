Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in Charleston

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever once a year to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on a descaling flush once a year if their water is hard.

Age matters too. Most tank water heaters run 8 to 12 years, tankless units often reach 20 with regular descaling, and heat pump models usually run 10 to 15. If your heater is past that window and showing symptoms such as discolored hot water, rumbling during heating, dampness under the tank, or hot water that runs out sooner than it used to, a planned replacement is nearly always less expensive and much easier on you than an emergency one with water on the floor.

Small problems rarely stay small when they're ignored. Take a spent anode rod: replacing it is a short, affordable repair, but if nobody replaces it, the water starts eating the tank steel, and suddenly a minor fix has become a full replacement. That's why we ask homeowners to keep our number in their phone and reach out at the first sign of trouble. Catching it early saves time and money and prevents a flooded utility room.

Typical Water Heater Issues in Charleston

Most water heater failures we see in Charleston start with what's in the water. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, hard water may be part of the picture: dissolved minerals settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the rumbling and popping you may hear during a heating cycle. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, which is why annual descaling is worth keeping up with where the water is hard.

Then there's age. When the sacrificial anode rod is gone, usually somewhere in the second half of a tank's life, the tank starts to rust from the inside, and a slow leak at the base is usually the first visible sign. The area has a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and elements, thermostats, and breakers on electric ones.

Homes on private wells can bring iron, sulfur, and grit into the tank, and a sulfur smell in the hot water often means a reaction with the anode rod that a swap to a different anode type can solve. Older homes often need code upgrades when a heater is replaced: an expansion tank where a check valve or pressure regulator creates a closed system, a correctly piped T&P discharge line, a drain pan where a leak could damage finished space, and seismic straps where local code requires them. Every one of these problems is fixable, and most cost far less to address early.
