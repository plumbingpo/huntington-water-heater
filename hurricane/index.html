Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in Hurricane

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is routine maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever once a year to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which reduces the risk of scalding and slows mineral buildup. Tankless owners should plan on descaling once a year in hard-water areas.

The age of your heater matters just as much. With regular service, a tank water heater usually runs 8 to 12 years, a heat pump water heater 10 to 15, and a tankless unit up to 20. Once a heater passes that range and starts acting up, like discolored hot water, popping noises, or a damp spot around the base, it's smart to start planning a replacement. Replacing on your own schedule costs less and is far less stressful than scrambling for a new heater with water spreading across the floor.

Minor issues don't stay minor for long. Consider a spent anode rod: replacing it is a short, inexpensive repair, but if nobody replaces it, the water starts eating the tank itself, and suddenly a minor fix becomes a full replacement. That's why we encourage homeowners to save our number in their phone and call at the first hint of trouble. Catching it early saves money and spares you a surprise cleanup.

Common Water Heater Problems in Hurricane

Water heaters in and around Hurricane deal with a familiar set of stresses. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, minerals can be a real factor: calcium and magnesium settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the popping and rumbling many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, and that's why regular descaling is worth keeping up with in hard-water areas.

Age is the other big factor. Plenty of homes around here still have the original tank, and beyond the ten-year mark, the anode rod has usually been consumed and corrosion begins attacking the steel from the inside. The area has a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, which means we service both sides: pilot assemblies, gas valves, and flues on gas units, and heating elements and thermostats on electric ones.

Every house is set up a little differently. A home on a private well may see more sediment and occasionally a sulfur smell in the hot water, often fixed by switching the anode rod type. A tankless heater on hard water should get annual descaling. And an older install being replaced may need updates it never had: an expansion tank, a correctly piped relief valve line, a drain pan, or strapping where required. All of these can be fixed, and most are much cheaper when caught early. If something seems off, a diagnostic visit from our team catches the small problem before it becomes an expensive one.
